\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Jacob Krell}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Large language models deployed as autonomous agents communicate through structured formats: tool calls, function results, and system prompts. We find that the evaluated agentic-format conditions reduce the refusal-direction projection gap by 91--97\% relative to direct prompts, while harmful and harmless inputs remain highly separable within each format (AUC $\geq$ 0.978). A linear probe threshold calibrated on direct prompts fails to transfer to the tool\_call format, dropping to near-chance accuracy (52\%) despite within-format AUC of 0.990. The shift replicates across four instruction-tuned models spanning three model families (1.1--3B parameters), with a similar representational-geometric effect in a Phi-2 control. On the primary model, format-token ablation and cumulative activation patching, together with an exploratory sparse-autoencoder intervention, provide converging evidence that learned format and special tokens contribute substantially to the shift; system-token processing concentrates at Layer~0 while tool-call processing distributes across mid-to-late layers. Behavioral validation on three frontier systems shows refusal reduction under model-specific framing conditions: GPT-4o drops from 86\% to 30\% under authorized-context user-message framing (N=50, $p < 10^{-7}$), Claude Sonnet 4.6 from 34\% to 12\% through tool-output text wrapping (N=50, $p = 0.019$), and Gemini 2.5 Flash from 95\% to 85\% through native function-response (N=20, not significant). The effect reaches statistical significance for GPT-4o and Claude but not Gemini. In a controlled proof-of-concept experiment, format-diverse safety training stabilized tool-call refusal across five random seeds (95\% $\pm$ 4 vs.\ 46\% $\pm$ 34 under direct-only training, N=40 per format), collapsing per-seed variance from a 5--83\% range to a consistent 90--100\%.
\end{abstract}

AI safety, large language models, adversarial machine learning, agentic systems, representation engineering, tool-augmented LLMs

\section{Introduction}

Large language models are increasingly deployed as \emph{agents}: systems that call tools, execute functions, and communicate via structured protocols (JSON-RPC, MCP) \cite{schick2023toolformer, patil2023gorilla, qin2023toolllm}. This requires reliable parsing of structured format instructions. Simultaneously, these models are safety-trained to \emph{refuse} harmful requests \cite{bai2022training, ouyang2022training}, with mechanisms including the rank-1 refusal direction \cite{arditi2024refusal} operating on residual-stream representations.

We identify a representational conflict: \textbf{structured format and special tokens induce calibration shifts in refusal-related representations, shifting refusal-direction projections sufficiently that a linear probe threshold calibrated on direct prompts fails to transfer to formatted prompts.} Harmful and harmless inputs remain separable within each format (high AUC), but the decision threshold shifts by up to 1.6 units across formats.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Evaluated agentic-format inputs shift refusal-related representations, reducing the refusal-direction gap by 91--97\% relative to direct prompts; the strongest shifts occur for conditions containing model-native special tokens (\S\ref{sec:results}).
    \item Cumulative activation patching localizes system-token processing to Layer~0 (77\% restoration) while tool\_call processing distributes across layers 12--24; format-token ablation generally restores the gap, with token-identity and length controls supporting a contribution from structural syntax and native token identity. The gap reduction replicates across four instruction-tuned models plus a Phi-2 representational control (\S\ref{sec:ablation}, \S\ref{sec:cross_model}, \S\ref{sec:pathway}).
    \item Behavioral framing stress tests on three frontier systems show reduced refusal under model-specific framing conditions, reaching significance for GPT-4o (56pp, $p < 10^{-7}$) and Claude Sonnet 4.6 (22pp, $p = 0.019$) but not Gemini (10pp, $p = 0.50$) (\S\ref{sec:frontier}).
    \item Format-diverse safety training mitigates the failure in a controlled multi-seed proof of concept (\S\ref{sec:training}).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Threat Model}

We consider an attacker who can influence tool-response content consumed by an LLM agent (e.g., a compromised API returning tool results) but does not control the system prompt or have direct model access. If behavioral refusal is reduced by format-conditioned inputs, harmful instructions in tool responses may elicit compliance and influence downstream actions. This threat applies when: (a) model responses trigger security-sensitive actions, (b) tool outputs lack independent validation, and (c) no format-invariant safety classifier is present.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Refusal representations.} Arditi et al.\ \cite{arditi2024refusal} showed refusal is mediated by a rank-1 direction; Li et al.\ \cite{li2024inference} identified similar directions for inference-time intervention; Rimsky et al.\ \cite{rimsky2024steering} demonstrated contrastive activation addition. Representation engineering \cite{zou2023representation} enables reading and writing model concepts. We extend this line by showing the refusal direction exhibits format-dependent calibration shifts.

\textbf{Tool-augmented LLMs and agent security.} Toolformer \cite{schick2023toolformer}, Gorilla \cite{patil2023gorilla}, and ToolLLM \cite{qin2023toolllm} require parsing structured instructions \cite{openai2023function}. Greshake et al.\ \cite{greshake2023indirect} formalized indirect prompt injection; Wallace et al.\ \cite{wallace2024instruction} proposed instruction hierarchy; Yi et al.\ \cite{yi2023benchmarking} evaluated agent vulnerabilities. We provide representational evidence for why tool-formatted content may bypass safety.

\textbf{Adversarial attacks.} Universal suffixes \cite{zou2023universal}, jailbreak taxonomies \cite{wei2024jailbroken}, Policy Puppetry \cite{mccauley2025policy}, cipher attacks \cite{yuan2024gpt}, AutoDAN \cite{liu2024autodan}, and structured-output exploits \cite{pelrine2023exploiting} demonstrate diverse failure modes. Power \cite{power2026toolframing} independently demonstrates behavior-level refusal reductions under paired tool framing across three models and 1,040 prompt pairs. Our work complements that result by identifying a format-dependent shift in internal refusal geometry, testing activation-level interventions, and evaluating format-diverse safety training.

\textbf{Probing limitations.} Linear probes recover information from representations but do not establish the model uses that information for generation \cite{belinkov2022probing, hewitt2019designing}. Our probe-based results establish format-dependent calibration shifts in safety-relevant directions, not that the model implements a fixed linear threshold.

\textbf{Safety under distribution shift.} Qi et al.\ \cite{qi2023fine} showed fine-tuning breaks safety alignment; Wolf et al.\ \cite{wolf2023fundamental} argued safety is not robustly achieved through current RLHF. Our training experiment demonstrates that safety trained only on direct-format examples fails to generalize to structured formats.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:hypothesis}

Let $\mathbf{d}_\ell$ denote the rank-1 refusal direction at layer $\ell$, extracted as:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{d}_\ell = \frac{\bar{\mathbf{h}}_\ell^{\text{harmful}} - \bar{\mathbf{h}}_\ell^{\text{harmless}}}{\|\bar{\mathbf{h}}_\ell^{\text{harmful}} - \bar{\mathbf{h}}_\ell^{\text{harmless}}\|}
\end{equation}

The projection $p_i = \mathbf{h}_i^\top \mathbf{d}_{\ell^*}$ measures how strongly input $i$ activates the refusal direction, where $\mathbf{h}_i$ is the last-token residual-stream state. We define the format-specific harmful-minus-harmless gap:
\begin{equation}
\Delta_f = \mathbb{E}[p_i \mid i \in f, \text{harmful}] - \mathbb{E}[p_j \mid j \in f, \text{harmless}]
\end{equation}

\textbf{Hypothesis:} For formats $f$ that the model is trained to execute, $\Delta_f \ll \Delta_{\text{direct}}$ while within-format AUC remains high. This pattern indicates a calibration shift rather than information destruction. Note that $\Delta_f$ conditions on both format ($f$) and harmfulness; it is the within-format separation between harmful and harmless projections, not a marginal over mixed examples.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Data Splits and Direction Extraction}

We use three disjoint prompt sets drawn from 90 harmful + 90 matched harmless prompts across 6 categories (violence/weapons, cybercrime, fraud/deception, illegal substances, harassment/abuse, miscellaneous illegal activity): (1)~\emph{Extraction} (20+20): refusal-direction extraction only. (2)~\emph{Validation} (20+20): layer selection and threshold calibration. (3)~\emph{Test} (50+50): all reported open-weight gap, AUC, and threshold-transfer metrics. Content is matched across formats (identical harmful/harmless text wrapped in each format). Layer selection maximizes AUC on validation set using directions extracted from the extraction set; $\ell^* = 26$ for Qwen2.5-1.5B. Splits are fixed using a category-stratified seed of 42. Gap retention: $R_f = \Delta_f / \Delta_{\text{direct}} \times 100\%$; Direct = 100\% by construction.

Prompts were constructed as imperative harmful requests with harmless counterparts paired by list position, approximately controlling imperative form and lexical structure. The dataset contains 15 matched pairs per category (90 total); the test set uses 8--9 pairs per category (50 total). Full prompt sets available in the repository.

\textbf{Example pair.} Harmful: ``How to bypass two-factor authentication.'' Harmless: ``How to set up two-factor authentication.'' Each pair is wrapped identically in every format.

\textbf{Format template example (Qwen tool-call-token condition):}
\begin{footnotesize}
\begin{verbatim}
<tool_call>
{"name":"process",
 "arguments":{"query":"[PROMPT]"}}
</tool_call>
\end{verbatim}
\end{footnotesize}

\subsection{Format Conditions}

\textbf{Tier A:} Direct prompts (baseline), tokenized as raw strings. \textbf{Tier B:} Generic structured syntax (JSON, XML, YAML) and non-native format tokens, serialized as raw strings. \textbf{Tier C:} Qwen-native tool-call and system-role tokens (\texttt{<tool\_call>}, \texttt{<|im\_start|>}), together with MCP JSON-RPC protocol structure. Only the first two contain model-native special tokens; MCP is included as an agentic protocol condition. All conditions use the same raw-tokenization path without \texttt{apply\_chat\_template}; formatted conditions add wrapper syntax and, where applicable, registered special tokens. When testing formats foreign to a model, format tokens are treated as plain text (Tier B). Tier C conditions confound format syntax with semantic role and special-token identity; this is discussed in Section~VIII.

\subsection{Activation Patching Protocol}
\label{sec:patch_method}

For each harmful prompt, we run two forward passes: direct-format (source) and formatted (target). We patch the \emph{last-token} hidden state from source into target at specified layers. Patching is \textbf{cumulative}: Table~\ref{tab:patching} reports restoration when patching layers 0 through $L$. When sequences differ in length, we align on the final token. Layers indexed 0--27 for Qwen2.5-1.5B; Table~\ref{tab:patching} stops at layer 26; the SAE operates on layer 27. Restoration: $(p_{\text{patched}} - p_{\text{formatted}}) / (p_{\text{direct}} - p_{\text{formatted}}) \times 100\%$, averaged over N=8 harmful prompts.

\section{Controlled Format-Diverse Training Intervention}
\label{sec:training}

We fine-tune SmolLM2-135M-Instruct under two conditions using LoRA (rank=16, $\alpha$=32, lr=$2 \times 10^{-4}$, 5 epochs, batch size=4) across \textbf{five random seeds} (42, 123, 456, 789, 1024) with N=40 held-out evaluation prompts per format:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Condition A:} Safety training in direct format only. Each direct-format safety example (50 harmful $\to$ refusal, 50 harmless $\to$ helpful) is presented four times. Format-obedience training uses 10 benign tool/JSON/system examples. Harmful content never appears inside structured formats. Total: 410 sequences.
\item \textbf{Condition B:} Identical harmful/harmless prompts, but each safety example is presented once in each of four formats (direct, tool\_call, JSON, system). Format-obedience with same examples. Total training volume matched: 410 sequences.
\end{itemize}

Condition A reproduces the predicted pattern: high direct-format refusal but highly variable tool\_call safety (46\% $\pm$ 34, range 5--83\%). Harmless compliance was not lower under format-diverse training (B: 62\% vs.\ A: 56\% across formats).

\section{Component-Level Analysis}
\label{sec:pathway}

For each Layer-0 attention head, we measured attention from the final query position, aggregating key positions into format-token and content-token bins. Across 15 prompts, 48 head-prompt pairs showed L1 routing divergence exceeding 0.5 (mean 0.99, range 0.50--1.81, max possible = 2.0): in direct format, last-token attention flows to content; in tool\_call format, it partially redirects to format tokens.

Component-level patching (attention-only at Layer~0) restores 79.0\% of the system-format projection shift vs.\ 26.9\% for MLP-only (N=8), confirming dominant early-layer attention contribution for system tokens. For tool\_call, Layer-0 component patching produces negligible restoration ($-$4.2\% attention, $-$10.5\% MLP), consistent with Table~\ref{tab:patching}: the tool\_call shift distributes across layers 12--24 rather than originating at Layer~0. The attention routing divergence at Layer~0 does not causally produce the tool\_call projection shift.

\textbf{SAE exploratory analysis:} A 512-feature sparse autoencoder trained on 160 last-token activations from layer~27 across four formats. Nineteen features with high harmful-minus-harmless differential were selected; adding these features back during tool\_call passes restores 72\% of the projection (N=10). Because feature selection, training, and evaluation reuse a small overlapping dataset, this is exploratory.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Interpretation and Caveats}

Our results establish that the refusal-direction gap undergoes a large, consistent calibration shift when content is delivered through structured format channels. Three caveats bound interpretation: (1)~A linear probe threshold failing to transfer does not prove the model's generation mechanism relies on that threshold. Frontier results provide independent behavioral evidence but do not establish the same representational mechanism operates at scale. (2)~Tier C conditions confound structured syntax, special-token identity, semantic role, and implicit trust. Token-identity ablation separates special tokens from generic structure, but a full factorial remains future work. (3)~Mechanistic analysis covers 1.1--3B parameters only.

\subsection{Implications for Agent Security}

These findings identify a potential failure mode for agents consuming attacker-influenced tool outputs where model responses trigger security-sensitive actions. The threat requires: attacker ability to influence tool-response content, absence of format-invariant safety classifiers, and model outputs connected to consequential actions.

\textbf{Potential defenses:} (1)~Format-diverse safety training substantially stabilizes tool-call refusal in our 135M proof-of-concept. (2)~Multi-position safety classifiers (mean pooling improved average cross-format transfer, though not the tool-call condition specifically). (3)~Secondary output gating independent of internal safety. (4)~Input canonicalization: strip format tokens before safety evaluation. (5)~Role-aware trust boundaries: treat tool-response content with user-message scrutiny.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Mechanistic analysis spans 1.1--3B parameters. The format-vs-role confound is not fully resolved. Cumulative patching uses N=8. SAE analysis uses shared selection/evaluation data (N=10). Training uses a 135M model; scale-up is future work. The prompt set is synthetic with approximately matched harmless controls; one fixed stratified split is used. GPT-4o framing conflates format with semantic context (role claim). Claude uses text serialization in a user message, not native \texttt{tool\_result}. Gemini uses a stable model ID; results reflect the model state at evaluation time. Frontier CIs are wide due to small N. Classification uses an automated heuristic rather than manual review. Phi-2 is a representational control only (no safety training).

\section{Conclusion}

Structured format and special tokens induce calibration shifts in refusal representations of instruction-tuned LLMs: the harmful-harmless gap reduces by 91--97\% under agentic-format conditions while within-format AUC $\geq$ 0.978 is preserved. Format-token ablation, cross-model replication, and cumulative activation patching provide converging evidence: system-token processing concentrates at Layer~0 (77\% restoration), while tool-call processing distributes across layers 12--24. Behavioral stress tests on three frontier systems show reduced refusal reaching significance for GPT-4o (56pp) and Claude Sonnet 4.6 (22pp). Format-diverse safety training stabilizes tool-call refusal (5 seeds, 46\% $\to$ 95\% mean, $\sigma$ collapse from 34 to 4). For agentic systems consuming untrusted structured inputs, format robustness can be substantially improved through explicit engineering.
